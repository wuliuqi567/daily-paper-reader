ABSTRACT 

Routing quantum keys over low-earth-orbit (LEO) satel-lite constellations is harder than classical routing: satel-lite handovers couple consecutive scheduling decisions, sto-chastic cloud cover can silently zero a ground link, and finite-key effects eliminate short, low-elevation passes en-tirely. We present SATLOCK, a handover-aware Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) routing framework that combines (i) a composite channel model incorporating atmospheric loss, pointing jitter, Markov cloud cover, decoy-state es-timation, and finite-key correction; (ii) an integer linear program (ILP) giving a provable handover-aware through-put upper bound; and (iii) a decentralized deep Q-network (DQN) baseline for weather-adaptive online routing. We evaluate two contention regimes on a Walker constellation serving intercontinental demands. In low contention (16 satellites, 6 demands), the ILP delivers 1,311 Mbit while the strongest heuristics reach 95–96% of ILP. In high contention (8 satellites, 12 demands), where handovers become bind-ing, heuristics drop to 89.5% of ILP. The DQN agent reaches 91.8% and 84.6% of ILP in the two regimes; it learns effective per-demand weather policies but is limited in aggregate by the lack of cross-demand coordination.

1 INTRODUCTION 

Routing cryptographic keys over low-earth-orbit (LEO) satellite constellations is qualitatively harder than classical traffic engineering. Quantum signals cannot be amplified or copied [17], so every lost photon is permanent. Each satel-lite pass lasts only a few minutes, stochastic cloud cover can silently zero a ground link mid-session, and finite-key overhead renders short low-elevation passes nearly use-less. Most critically, satellite handovers —reassigning a satel-lite to a different demand between consecutive time slots— incur a pointing-and-acquisition penalty that couples ad-jacent scheduling decisions in a way classical networks do not face. Ignoring this temporal coupling, as existing heuristics do [10], leads to measurable throughput loss that grows with constellation contention. Quantum key dis-tribution (QKD) exploits quantum no-cloning to generate information-theoretically secure shared keys [3]. LEO satel-lite links traverse the thin upper atmosphere, enabling in-tercontinental key exchange beyond the reach of terrestrial fiber—demonstrated by the Micius mission [8], and now con-sidered the most credible near-term path to a global quan-tum network [12]. Yet no prior routing work simultane-ously accounts for finite-key effects, stochastic weather, and handover-coupled scheduling. Three terms recur through-out the paper. Finite-key effects are the security penalty in-curred because a real pass yields only a finite number of detected signals, so the usable key rate falls below the as-ymptotic value and vanishes for very short passes. Cloud cover acts as an on/off mask: a clouded ground station re-ceives no photons, regardless of geometry. A handover is arXiv:2607.17076v1 [cs.CR] 19 Jul 2026 QuNet ’26, September 2026, Denver, CO, USA Hossain and Hossain 

the reassignment of a satellite from one ground demand to another between consecutive slots, forcing the optical termi-nal to re-point and re-acquire and thus lose part of a slot— the effect that couples scheduling decisions across time and that prior routing work has not modeled. We present SAT-LOCK , a handover-coupled scheduling framework for LEO QKD networks. Its core insight is that handover penalties create temporal coupling between consecutive slot decisions: a globally optimal schedule avoids unnecessary satellite re-assignments, while myopic per-slot heuristics do not. We quantify this gap with a provable ILP upper bound and show it reaches 10.5% under high contention which is a difference that matters for a technology where every secure bit is ex-pensive.

2 RELATED WORK 

Liao et al. [8] and Yin et al. [18] demonstrated satellite QKD and entanglement distribution at intercontinental range, establishing the physical link model we adopt. Beding-ton et al. [2] and Ribezzo et al. [14] identify pointing loss, cloud outages, and finite-key overhead as dominant im-pairments, with cloud-driven outages confirmed as the pri-mary bottleneck in a deployed two-node network, but none addresses multi-demand routing or handover scheduling. Mehic et al. [10], Cao et al. [5], and Aguado et al. [1] study trusted-node routing and SDN control for QKD over fiber, assuming deterministic links and asymptotic key rates. Cal-effi [4] and Pant et al. [11] propose fidelity- and success-probability-based routing for quantum repeater networks, assuming static link qualities without visibility windows or handover penalties; we reuse their criteria as baselines in Section 5, where both stay within a few percent of opti-mal under low contention but, scoring each slot indepen-dently, lose ground once handover coupling makes the best per-slot choice differ from the best schedule—precisely the regime this paper targets. SATLOCK is the first framework to jointly model finite-key correction, Markov cloud cover, and handover-coupled scheduling with a provable optimal-ity certificate. Classical LEO scheduling optimizes throughput under de-terministic or i.i.d. channel models and ignores quantum-specific handover irreversibility, since a handover penalty in a QKD slot cannot be recovered as classical retransmission allows; SATLOCK ’s ILP captures this via temporal coupling constraints absent in classical scheduling. DRL has been ap-plied to traffic engineering [16] and wireless resource man-agement [7], but without handover coupling or correlated quantum channels; our DQN agent instead trains under Markov-correlated weather, includes a handover flag to ex-pose coupling cost, and uses cumulative key progress for demand-aware reallocation, leaving the residual gap to ILP as motivation for centralized multi-agent DRL. 

2.1 Satellite Constellation and Ground Network 

We model a Walker 53 °:16/4/1 constellation (inclina-tion:total/planes/phasing notation): 4 orbital planes at 53 °

inclination, 500 km altitude, 4 satellites per plane, 𝑁 𝑠 = 16 to-tal, with 90 ° inter-plane RAAN spacing. The ground network covers 10 stations across East Asia (Beijing, Tokyo, Seoul, Shanghai), South Asia (Mumbai), Southeast Asia (Singapore, Bangkok), and Europe (Vienna, London, Madrid). A satellite is visible above 10 ° elevation ( ≈2100 km footprint radius); time is discretized into 𝑇 = 60 slots of 2 min over a 2-hour orbital window. The LEO satellite layer serves as a quantum relay tier, forwarding key material between trusted ground nodes via direct or 1-hop relay paths. Each satellite is a 

trusted-node relay rather than a quantum repeater: it termi-nates a downlink, measures the received key material, and re-transmits to the next hop, so no end-to-end entanglement is preserved across the relay. Each satellite also operates a single steerable downlink and therefore serves at most one ground station per slot—the physical origin of the exclusiv-ity constraint in Section 3, where multi-beam payloads that lift this restriction are discussed as a relaxation.

2.3 Qiskit BB84 Validation 

We validate the channel model using a prepare-and-measure BB84 simulation in Qiskit with depolarizing noise [13]. For each channel error rate in [0, 0.20 ], we simulate 500 qubit transmissions per trial over 5 independent trials. The mea-sured quantum bit error rate (QBER) tracks the theoretical relationship QBER ≈ 𝑒 channel within the expected sampling uncertainty of the finite-shot simulation: at QBER = 5% , the per-trial standard error is √︁ 0.05 × 0.95 /500 ≈ 1.0% , falling to ≈ 0.44% averaged over five trials. We therefore treat the Qiskit run as a protocol-level consistency check rather than a model of QKD hardware—a noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) device and a real photonic transmitter differ substantially, and the simulation only confirms that the prepare-and-measure protocol reproduces the expected QBER-versus-error relationship our channel model assumes. The finite-key rate (3) computed from simulated QBER re-mains within 1–2% of the analytical prediction for passes above 60 s, confirming end-to-end consistency between the protocol layer and the network-level channel model.

5.2 Per-Demand and Algorithm Comparison 

Under Markov-correlated weather and handover loss (30 held-out seeds), the algorithms separate clearly. In low contention (16 satellites, 6 demands), the strongest heuris-tics remain close to ILP since satellite exclusivity is only occasionally binding; even there, the handover-aware ILP delivers the largest aggregate key volume (1,311 Mbit, vs. 1,262 Mbit for Caleffi/Pant, 1,250 Mbit for Greedy, and 1,204 Mbit for DRL; Table 3). The per-demand break-down in Figure 3, computed under the same Markov weather and handover loss, also exposes a fairness behavior: since (4) maximizes total delivered key volume, it favors less-impaired demand pairs—visible in the large gap between high-volume pairs like Beijing–Seoul and the near-zero de-livery to Tokyo–Shanghai—and can leave the weakest un-derserved under high contention. We optimize aggregate throughput because delivered bits are the security-relevant resource and total throughput is a single well-defined cer-tifiable target; this trades per-demand fairness, and could in principle starve a persistently cloud-blocked station. A fairness-aware variant—e.g., max-min or proportionally fair objectives, or a per-demand minimum-key constraint—is a natural extension, likely trading some aggregate volume for more even delivery; we leave its evaluation to future work. The separation is larger under high contention (8 satellites, 12 demands), where satellite conflicts and handovers occur more frequently: as Figure 4 shows under the same 30-seed evaluation, the ILP delivers 980 Mbit, the myopic baselines drop to 877 Mbit (89.5% of ILP), and the decentralized DRL agent delivers 829 Mbit (84.6%; Table 3). Here, constraint C2 binds at nearly every slot, forcing all myopic algorithms to the same feasible assignment and identical throughput, vis-ible as matching bar heights across baselines for most de-mand pairs in the figure. 5.4 Discussion 

The handover-aware ILP solves in ∼0.8 s at this scale (64+ satellites would need column-generation or Lagrangian de-composition [12]), and handover loss couples adjacent deci-sions, exposing the value of global scheduling over per-slot selection. The DRL agent infers in ∼50 𝜇 s per slot without re-solving an NP-hard program, but its independent-agent de-sign cannot fully resolve centralized satellite conflicts; per-demand agents avoid the combinatorial joint action space (3𝐾 ) and specialize to each pair’s conditions at this coor-dination cost, while precomputing the best path per type and choosing only among types reduces the action space to 3 discrete actions, enabling convergence within 3,000 episodes, and including cumulative key progress 𝑏 𝑡 and a handover flag 𝑔 𝑡 in the state enables demand-aware alloca-tion that redirects capacity to underserved pairs once oth-ers are satisfied—insights that may transfer to other quan-tum network problems with combinatorial path spaces and stochastic channels. Our evaluation remains simulation-based, with cloud cover drawn from a two-state Markov process using representative climatological parameters rather than measured traces—the study’s main limitation, and validat-ing against real meteorological traces and recorded pass geometries is our immediate next step, with centralized or communication-enabled multi-agent DRL as the natural next step for closing the gap to ILP. 

6 CONCLUSION 

We presented SATLOCK , a handover-coupled scheduling framework for LEO QKD networks combining a physi-cally grounded channel model (finite-key correction, decoy-state estimation, Markov weather, pointing jitter, and han-dover loss) with a provable ILP optimality certificate and a weather-adaptive online routing agent. The evaluation shows clear algorithm differentiation: the best heuristics reach 95–96% of ILP under low contention but drop to 89.5% under high contention, as satellite conflicts and handover penalties become binding; the decentralized DRL agent learns useful weather-sensitive demand policies, but its ag-gregate performance is limited by independent per-demand coordination. Future work includes centralized multi-agent DRL, multi-hop entanglement routing with quantum re-peaters, real ECMWF cloud-cover integration per station, and hardware-in-the-loop validation using SDR testbeds or emulated QKD devices.
